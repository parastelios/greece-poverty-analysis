The Greek Poverty Paradox

Since 2015, Greece has recorded the EU's highest rate of reported difficulty making ends meet: roughly two in three households. Yet the official income-poverty rate places Greece seventh-highest among the 27 member states, a serious but far less exceptional position. This project examines what lies behind the average 52.6-percentage-point gap between those two measures and how much of it the available evidence can explain.

The question behind the numbers

There's a strange inversion in Greece's public economic debate. When unemployment falls or GDP rises, the indicators are presented as proof of recovery. But when two in three households say they're struggling to make ends meet, attention often shifts from the indicators to households' own habits: coffee, takeout, travel, or Greek pessimism.

Is that shift justified? The official income-poverty rate places Greece in a difficult, but not exceptional, position in Europe. Households' own answers put it in a category nearly of its own. This project starts from that contradiction and examines two questions:

01

Is the hardship real, or just Greek pessimism?

Do households' own answers track specific material difficulties, or are they mainly a different way of answering?

02

If the hardship is real, why doesn't the official picture of recovery capture it?

What do present-day conditions add, what does the accumulated weight of the crisis add, and what remains unexplained?

    What the analysis finds. Reported hardship cannot be dismissed as pessimism alone. It moves with specific material difficulties and with broader pressures involving wages, purchasing power, unemployment and housing.

    No single factor, however, robustly explains the full 52.6-percentage-point average gap. The results show associations between countries, not that any one factor causes hardship within individual households.
